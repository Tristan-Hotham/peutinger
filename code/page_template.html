Peutinger Table

A Roman map of the roads from Britain to India, squeezed onto a strip of parchment {{source.parchment_m|f1}} metres long and {{source.parchment_cm_high|int}} centimetres high. I found {{located.n|int}} of its places by eye, set the scroll beside a modern map, and measured {{dist.n_gc|int}} of its road legs. The shape is wildly wrong. The distances, mostly, are right.

In Vienna, in the Austrian National Library, lies a medieval copy of a Roman map. It is made of eleven sheets of parchment glued end to end into a strip about {{source.parchment_m|f1}} metres long and only {{source.parchment_cm_high|int}} centimetres high. On it the whole Roman world, from the Rhine mouth to Sri Lanka, is pressed flat like a flower in a book. Seas become thin ribbons. Italy lies on its side. Rivers run along the strip instead of across it. Yet the map is covered in roads, drawn as red lines with a little kink at every stopping place, and beside each stretch a number: how far to the next station.

The first sheet, which showed Spain and most of Britain, was lost long ago. What survives is known as the Peutinger Table, the Tabula Peutingeriana, after Konrad Peutinger, the Augsburg scholar who owned it in the sixteenth century. Richard Talbert's modern edition identifies its places, and the OmnesViae project by René Voorburg turns it into a dataset of {{source.tp|int}} stations and {{source.legs_surv|int}} legs on the surviving sheets, with the lost western end rebuilt from another Roman road book. I used that dataset, found {{located.n|int}} of the places by eye on Konrad Miller's 1887 facsimile, and set the scroll above a modern map. Click a place on either map to see it on the other.

    02 · DistancesWrong shape, right numbers

    The numbers on the Tabula are not labelled with a unit, and the OmnesViae dataset stores them as plain numbers. Scholars read most of them as Roman miles of about {{units.rm_km|f2}} km, and those in Gaul as Gallic leagues of one and a half miles. The map itself drops a hint: beside Lyon it says "Lugduno caput Galliarum usque hic legas", Lyon, capital of the Gauls, leagues up to here.

    The measurements back this up. For the {{units.Mile.n|int}} measurable legs outside Gaul, each unit on the map covers a median {{units.Mile.km_per_unit_gc|f2}} km as the crow flies and {{units.Mile.km_per_unit_road|f2}} km along the Roman road network mapped by the Itiner-e project. In Aquitania, Lugdunensis and Belgica, the three Gallic provinces beyond Narbonensis, and in the two Germanies, the same unit covers {{units.League.km_per_unit_gc|f2}} km as the crow flies, {{units.league_over_mile|f2}} times as much: very close to the one and a half of a league. In Narbonensis, the old Roman province on the Mediterranean, the figure falls straight back to {{units.narbonensis.km_per_unit_gc|f2}}. Read as miles, Gaul's roads would come out {{units.gaul_as_miles_err|f2}} times longer than the figures say; read as leagues, the ratio is {{units.gaul_as_leagues_err|f2}}, right to within a few per cent.

    Converted that way, the Tabula's distances hold up. Across the {{dist.n_road|int}} legs where both stations sit on a mapped Roman road, the median road distance is {{dist.road_long_pct|int}}% longer than the figure on the map, and the median straight line {{dist.gc_short_pct|int}}% shorter, so the written figures sit just where a road distance should. {{dist.road_within25_pct|int}}% of legs agree with the road to within 25%, and {{dist.road_within50_pct|int}}% to within 50%. The misses are the familiar faults of a copy of a copy: a numeral dropped or doubled, a station skipped, a road joined to the wrong town.

    Over long journeys the errors cancel. Adding up the Tabula's own figures from Rome, the road to Cologne comes to {{routes.Cologne.km|int}} km, against {{routes.Cologne.road_km|int}} km along the mapped Roman roads; to Vienna {{routes.Vienna.km|int}} against {{routes.Vienna.road_km|int}}; to Constantinople {{routes.Constantinople.km|int}} against {{routes.Constantinople.road_km|int}}. The farther east, the more the Tabula's totals run short of the mapped roads: by about an eighth on the way to Antioch ({{routes.Antioch.km|int}} against {{routes.Antioch.road_km|int}}) and Jerusalem ({{routes.Jerusalem.km|int}} against {{routes.Jerusalem.road_km|int}}).

    The far east is different. In Persia and India, where the map goes beyond anything Rome governed, only {{units.Far east.n|int}} legs can be measured, and one unit stands for anything from {{units.far_east_min|f1}} to {{units.far_east_max|f1}} km. Some look like Roman miles, some like Persian parasangs of five or six kilometres, and the Indian ones fit nothing. The map's makers were copying figures they could not check.

    03 · The squeezeA world pressed flat

    The places I located run from {{shape.west|raw}} (Brest) in the west to {{shape.east|raw}} (Patna) in the east, about {{shape.ew_km|int}} km, and from {{shape.north|raw}} on the Rhine mouth to {{shape.south|raw}} in south India, about {{shape.ns_km|int}} km. The real world they cover is {{shape.real_ratio|f1}} times as wide as it is tall. The scroll is {{shape.scroll_ratio|f1}} times as wide as it is tall. So one centimetre of parchment stands, on average, for {{shape.km_per_cm_ew|f1}} km east to west but {{shape.km_per_cm_ns|f1}} km north to south: the world is squashed about {{shape.stretch_whole|f1}} times more in one direction than the other.

    Still, the order is kept. West to east along the scroll follows west to east on the ground almost perfectly: the rank correlation between a place's longitude and its position along the scroll is {{shape.x_spearman_lon|f2}}. North to south is much looser ({{shape.y_spearman_lat|f2}} for latitude against height on the scroll), because whole regions are turned on their sides to fit. Italy runs along the scroll from its north-west to its south-east, and so does North Africa, laid out beneath it like a second long peninsula.

    Region by region, the squeeze varies. North Africa is the most stretched ({{regions.North Africa.aniso|int}} times), a long ribbon of coast with almost no depth; the Danube lands come next ({{regions.Alps and Danube.aniso|int}} times), then Egypt, Greece and Italy. Asia Minor ({{regions.Asia Minor.aniso|f1}}) and the Levant ({{regions.Levant.aniso|f1}}) keep more of their shape, and Mesopotamia ({{regions.Mesopotamia and Armenia.aniso|f1}}) and Persia and India ({{regions.Persia, Central Asia and India.aniso|f1}}) least of all, but only because there the map hardly tries: the far east gets a couple of sheets of loosely placed names.

    Turn on "Modern coastline, bent to fit" in the scroll's Map layers to see this. I fitted a smooth surface through the {{located.n|int}} located places and used it to bend Natural Earth's coastline onto the scroll. The real Adriatic and Tyrrhenian coasts of Italy land almost exactly on the Tabula's grey-green seas.

    04 · SpaceWho gets the parchment

    Giving each part of the scroll to its nearest located place, Italy and its islands take {{regions.Italy and islands.scroll_pct|f1}}% of the surviving parchment, though they are only {{regions.Italy and islands.land_pct|f1}}% of the land of the empire's provinces: {{regions.Italy and islands.space_ratio|f1}} times their share. Mesopotamia and Armenia get {{regions.Mesopotamia and Armenia.space_ratio|f1}} times theirs, Greece and Thrace {{regions.Greece and Thrace.space_ratio|f1}}, the Levant {{regions.Levant.space_ratio|f1}}. Gaul ({{regions.Gaul and the Rhine.space_ratio|f2}}), North Africa ({{regions.North Africa.space_ratio|f2}}) and Asia Minor ({{regions.Asia Minor.space_ratio|f2}}) get about their fair share. Egypt gets far less ({{regions.Egypt and Cyrene.space_ratio|f2}}), but most of its province is empty desert, and the Tabula, like the Romans, cared about the Nile valley.

    Britain and Spain barely appear: the sheet that held them is lost, leaving only a corner of Kent and a sliver of Gaul's Atlantic coast. OmnesViae rebuilds that missing end from the Antonine Itinerary, another Roman road book, adding {{recon.stations|int}} stations and {{recon.legs|int}} legs. I keep those separate throughout. Their distances behave just like the Tabula's (median straight line {{recon.gc_median|f2}} of the written figure), as you would expect from a list of the same roads.

    05 · RomeThe middle of the world?

    Rome sits enthroned in a gold circle, one of only three cities drawn as a person, with Constantinople and Antioch. {{rome.degree|int}} roads leave it. But on the surviving scroll it is not in the middle: it lies {{rome.frac|f1}}% of the way from the left edge, in Miller's segment {{rome.seg|raw}}. The middle of the surviving scroll falls in the Balkans, near {{rome.middle_modern|raw}}. Add back one lost sheet of average width at the western end, and Rome moves to {{rome.frac_one_lost|f1}}%. It would sit at the exact centre only if about {{rome.lost_for_centre|f1}} sheets were lost; with {{rome.lost_for_centre_int|int}}, it lands at {{rome.frac_k_lost|f1}}%. Some scholars, Richard Talbert among them, argue that the map was designed with Rome at its centre. If so, the measurements show how much is missing: about three sheets, not one.

    06 · The whole road{{total.km_rounded_k|int}},000 kilometres

    Adding up every figure on the surviving sheets gives {{total.units|int}} units over {{total.legs|int}} legs: {{total.rm_mile_zone|int}} Roman miles, {{total.leagues|int}} Gallic leagues, and {{total.far_units|int}} units of unknown length in the far east. Converted, that is about {{total.km|int}} km of road, roughly {{total.pct_of_itinere|int}}% of the {{total.network_km|int}} km of Roman roads that Itiner-e has mapped. Another {{total.nodist_legs|int}} legs on the surviving scroll have no figure at all, and the lost west would add about {{recon.stated_km|int}} km more.

    Walking all of it, at the 20 to 25 km a day that Wikipedia gives for a traveller on foot, would take about {{total.days.foot|int}} days, close to {{total.years_foot|int}} years. A courier riding the imperial relay of horses, about 80 km a day, would need {{total.days.courier|int}} days.

    07 · How I did thisMethods and caveats

    The data. OmnesViae's export of the Tabula: {{source.places|int}} stations (one listed twice) and {{source.legs|int}} legs, with the name as written on the map, Talbert's identification and coordinates. I used its coordinates as given. Checking them against the Pleiades gazetteer of ancient places, {{source.pleiades_10km_pct|int}}% of the {{source.pleiades_checked|int}} stations whose name matches a Pleiades place lie within 10 km of it. East of the Euphrates many identifications are uncertain.

    Places on the scroll. There is no table of where each station sits on the Tabula, so I found {{located.n|int}} by eye on Miller's facsimile, reading the labels at full resolution and marking the town's picture or the start of its label: {{located.high|int}} read clearly and {{located.medium|int}} with some doubt. They cover every sheet, at least {{located.min_per_segment|int}} per sheet, but favour legible and well-known places.

    Units and distances. A leg's figure is converted at {{units.rm_km|f2}} km per Roman mile, or {{units.league_km|f2}} km per league in Aquitania, Lugdunensis, Belgica and the two Germanies. Straight-line distances are great circles between OmnesViae's coordinates. Road distances follow the Itiner-e network of Roman roads (from my earlier roman-roads study): each station is placed on the nearest road within {{dist.snap_km|int}} km, and the shortest route between the two is kept when it is no more than twice the straight line plus 10 km. That gave {{dist.n_road|int}} legs. Itiner-e's roads are digitised with their bends, and the route between two stations can take a detour where the network has a gap, so the road figures lean long.

    Shape and space. Stretch is the ratio of the two scale factors of a straight-line (affine) fit from ground to scroll, region by region; the fits are rough, since nothing on this map is straight. Share of the scroll gives each pixel of the painted strip to the region of its nearest located place, so it depends on where I located places. Land shares use the provinces of about AD 117 from the Digital Atlas of the Roman Empire, and are not defined beyond the frontier. Sizes in centimetres assume the surviving parchment is {{source.parchment_m|f2}} m long, the length usually quoted.

    Journeys. Shortest routes over all legs, weighted by the written distance; {{network.legs_filled|int}} legs with no figure are weighted by the road distance, the straight line times {{network.road_gc|f2}}, or a typical leg of {{network.median_leg_km|f1}} km. Days use Wikipedia's speeds: 22.5 km a day on foot, 45 by cart, 80 by courier relay.
