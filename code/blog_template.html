The Peutinger Table is a Roman road map squeezed onto a strip of parchment {{source.parchment_m|f1}} metres long and {{source.parchment_cm_high|int}} centimetres high. I found {{located.n|int}} of its places by eye, set it beside a modern map and measured its roads. The shape is hopeless. The numbers are not.

In the Austrian National Library in Vienna lies a medieval copy of a Roman map: eleven sheets of parchment glued into one long strip. On it the whole Roman world, and some of the world beyond, from the mouth of the Rhine to the tip of India, is pressed flat like a flower in a book. The seas are thin grey-green ribbons. Italy lies on its side. Yet the map is covered in roads, red lines with a little kink at every stopping place, and beside each stretch of road is a number: how far to the next station.

The OmnesViae project by René Voorburg, built on Richard Talbert's edition of the map, turns it into a dataset of {{source.tp|int}} stations and {{source.legs_surv|int}} road legs on the surviving sheets. I used that, found {{located.n|int}} of the places on Konrad Miller's 1887 facsimile, and put the scroll and a modern map side by side.

A world pressed flat

The places I located span about {{shape.ew_km|int}} km east to west and {{shape.ns_km|int}} km north to south. On the parchment, one centimetre covers about {{shape.km_per_cm_ew|f1}} km east to west but {{shape.km_per_cm_ns|f1}} km north to south: the world is squashed about {{shape.stretch_whole|int}} times more in one direction than the other. Some regions suffer more than others. North Africa becomes a ribbon of coast {{regions.North Africa.aniso|int}} times more stretched one way than the other; Italy, laid along the strip from north-west to south-east, about {{regions.Italy and islands.aniso|int}} times.

To see this I fitted a smooth surface through the located places and used it to bend today's coastline onto the scroll. The real coasts of Italy land almost exactly on the Tabula's seas.

But the numbers are right

The figures on the map carry no unit. Scholars read them as Roman miles, except in Gaul, where they are Gallic leagues of one and a half miles; beside Lyon the map itself says "usque hic legas", leagues up to here. The measurements agree. Outside Gaul, one unit covers a median {{units.Mile.km_per_unit_gc|f2}} km as the crow flies. In Gaul north of Lyon and the Rhineland it covers {{units.League.km_per_unit_gc|f2}} km, {{units.league_over_mile|f2}} times as much. Read as miles, Gaul's figures would be far too short; read as leagues, they are right to within a few per cent.

{{IMG|b_units.png|Dot chart of kilometres per unit on the map for each Roman province: Belgica, Aquitania, Lugdunensis and the two Germanies cluster around 2.1 to 2.3 km, close to a dashed line for the Gallic league, while every other province sits between about 1.2 and 1.7 km, around a dashed line for the Roman mile at 1.48 km|How much ground one unit on the map covers, province by province.}}

Converted that way, I compared every leg I could with the road network of the Itiner-e atlas of Roman roads. For {{dist.n_road|int}} legs, the median road is {{dist.road_long_pct|int}}% longer than the figure on the map and the median straight line {{dist.gc_short_pct|int}}% shorter, just where a road distance should sit. {{dist.road_within25_pct|int}}% agree with the road to within 25%. Over long journeys the errors cancel: by the Tabula's own figures it is {{routes.Vienna.km|int}} km from Rome to Vienna, against {{routes.Vienna.road_km|int}} km along the mapped roads, and {{routes.Cologne.km|int}} to Cologne, against {{routes.Cologne.road_km|int}}.

Who gets the parchment, and where is Rome?

Italy takes {{regions.Italy and islands.scroll_pct|f1}}% of the surviving scroll for {{regions.Italy and islands.land_pct|f1}}% of the empire's land, {{regions.Italy and islands.space_ratio|f1}} times its share. Mesopotamia, Greece and the Levant are also generous; Gaul, North Africa and Asia Minor get about their fair share. Rome itself, enthroned in a gold circle, is not in the middle of what survives: it sits {{rome.frac|int}}% of the way along. The first sheet is lost, but one lost sheet is not enough to centre it. It would take about {{rome.lost_for_centre_int|int}}.

As it survives Rome is 35% along; with one lost sheet added at the left, 41%; with three, 49%, almost exactly in the middle|Rome sits in the middle only if about three sheets are lost.}}

{{total.km_rounded_k|int}},000 kilometres of road

Adding up every figure on the surviving sheets gives about {{total.km|int}} km of road. Walking it, at the 20 to 25 km a day a Roman traveller managed on foot, would take {{total.days.foot|int}} days, close to {{total.years_foot|int}} years. By the map's own figures, Rome to Constantinople is {{routes.Constantinople.foot|int}} days on foot and Rome to Jerusalem {{routes.Jerusalem.foot|int}}. The Tabula draws no sea routes, so Carthage, a few days away by ship, is {{routes.Carthage.km|int}} km by its roads, the long way round the whole eastern Mediterranean.

How I did this

The stations, their identifications and coordinates, and the legs with their figures are from OmnesViae, after Talbert's edition; I used them as given. The {{located.n|int}} positions on the scroll are my own, read off Miller's facsimile, so they favour legible, well-known places. Units are assigned by province. Road distances follow the Itiner-e network, which includes every bend of the road, so they lean long. The lost western sheet, which OmnesViae rebuilds from another Roman road book, the Antonine Itinerary, is kept separate throughout. The paper sets out the methods and their limits in full.
